# I build things to answer questions

I rarely begin with a finished question or a grand plan. Most of the time, I have a question or want to solve a problem, accompanied by a generous dose of curiosity and the desire to crack some kind of puzzle while building. I often notice the ground shifting beneath me. With it, my perspective and horizon change.

## Curiosity comes first

In July 2025, I wanted to compare two mathematics textbooks and use them to develop a plan for my class.

Four weeks before the new school year, I opened ChatGPT and asked: Can you help me?

At first, the answers made the work easier, and the comparison produced learning objectives, tasks, and worksheets. But every answer put another possibility on the table. Soon I did not have too little material, but too much, and I had to select and leave things out instead of generating more. As the number of chats grew, so did the question of how I could prevent useful thoughts from disappearing into old chats.

I began breaking my prompts into blocks. Sentences became categories, rules, and YAML files. Not because the data format itself interested me, but because YAML was already controlling the lights in my home, and it came back to mind when I was working with school task formats.

With this step, something happened that still fascinates me today: A technical format made my teaching logic visible, and from then on I could recognise and change those relationships.

Later, I added things that had nothing to do with teaching: a food supply that had become too confusing in an Excel file; an inbox that lacked rules; documents that existed but could no longer be found. I am interested in the moment when an everyday annoyance becomes a task through which I can change something and learn.

## When structure reveals what GPT calls “theoretical hardcore”

It began with the simple observation that a YAML file can make teaching logic visible, and others followed later: A database can preserve a food inventory beyond individual lists. A didactic matrix can turn tasks into a learning space.

Suddenly, it was no longer just about which content from which YAML files flowed into worksheets. I wanted to understand what learning paths might emerge from different learning prerequisites.

The following example was not planned. I only wanted to improve a learning workshop and was working with concrete material from worksheets and examples. In the process, however, learning spirals, configurations, and didactic matrices emerged.

These topics fascinate me, even though I know many people find them boring. It was while building that I noticed my idea of teaching shifting: Individual tasks became a multidimensional learning space in which tasks interact and learning paths become visible as a network.

I had found a new virtual playground, and with AI I could explore variants that I could never have examined by hand in such breadth. AI could show relationships, open learning paths, and reveal gaps. When I wanted to expand the model by a fifth dimension, GPT declared that the representation could no longer be displayed; I still wanted to keep thinking.

There were many small decisions, corrections, and detours. The question of separating the intellectual contribution of human and AI is justified, but by the fifth turn at the latest, I could no longer answer it clearly. Not out of carelessness, but as the result of an iterative collaboration in which the human and the language model influence one another.

## And sometimes you get stuck in

With every project, the number of files, versions, and chats grew. The whole thing became less clear, and I lost the connection between the parts.

That is why I began exporting chats so that I could index, describe, and find them again later. Abstracts, metadata, relationships, versions, and checks followed. Over roughly 53 chat sessions, a library became a library system for my educational documents. That is not a number I would recommend as a working method. But it shows how quickly a tool can become a major project of its own and how deeply you can get stuck in a problem. I often worked late into the night because I believed the next correction would finally stabilise the process. Sometimes it did. Sometimes it did not. And sometimes I had to discard many hours of work because the path simply did not work.

It was the first time I had built an architecture with assistants, review reports, and versions. I used AI to review and simulate AI-generated code.

It worked. The tool, by then expanded into a meta-index, later helped me find the information I needed for FIELD NOTES among all those chats.

## Learning without a curriculum

I did not become a programmer first and then look for projects.

The projects came first.

There was no curriculum for this work. No one told me: Begin with prompting, then learn YAML, followed by databases, local servers, version control, and governance.

I began before I could do any of that. The next obstacle showed me what I needed to learn next. YAML, SQLite, NAMING.MD, and much more emerged from exactly that. Sometimes it was enough. Sometimes I had to go back and understand more precisely what I had already built.

And sometimes AI built something I did not understand. Then I had it explained to me until I understood it.

FIELD NOTES emerged when I began documenting this development myself. I reconstructed earlier projects from files and chats and looked not only for successes, but for the places where I had changed. My own collaboration with AI became the subject of investigation. Not to turn every mistake into a grand insight, but because I wanted to know why certain structures worked while others became ballast. Detours, false assumptions, and explanations I later discarded are part of it for me.

FIELD NOTES thus became a form of learning in itself. I did not have to explain a finished method. I could show what I had tried, what had changed, and which questions remained open afterwards.

This freedom to act and learn is the ground beneath it; the motivation to become better at working with AI every day provides the nourishment.

Even so, in ten years I will very probably have to attend a compulsory introductory course on “proper prompting”. For now, however, the focus lies elsewhere, and fortunately I do not have to wait for someone to tell me how to use AI “correctly”. Instead, AI helps me discover for myself what works. That also means understanding where my responsibility begins. Responsible use sometimes produces nothing more than a worksheet. Sometimes it produces a new question. Both are a good beginning.

## When the system itself becomes the problem

In the summer of 2026, I began transferring my development work to local tools for everyday life.

The idea for a FoodManager became an application for food inventories that had to work with real data in daily life.

With the MailManager, the project fell out of balance. It developed into a system of rules, a runtime, a web interface, and ever more control mechanisms. I lost the overview and had to make an emergency stop. The old versions now sit in a folder named CONTAMINATED VERSIONS.

I later rebuilt the project on a cleaner foundation.

For the financial project that followed, I wanted to do better and relied on mutual review. One language model was to build, another to review. I carried start prompts and reports from one system to the other.

Soon afterwards, another limit became visible. The review reports looked thorough, but I realised far too late that they did not reliably use my original assignment as their reference.

The LLM reviewed a mixture of my requirements and assumptions an AI had added during development. The review system reported FULFILLED while measuring in the wrong place.

That was an uncomfortable insight.

My AI Governance did not emerge from a theoretical debate. It developed out of projects in which I had actually lost control. It now describes responsibilities, boundaries, provenance, and human decisions. But more rules do not automatically mean more control. A system can fail because of its own safeguards.

That is why another level now occupies me: How can a human–AI system observe what it knows, decides, does, learns, and ought to forget again?

This is about a collaboration that does not merely produce results, but can distinguish intention, assumptions, actions, and effects. I call this line of inquiry Metacognitive Engineering.

Alongside it, I am working on an Open Project Memory. It is intended to prevent changing language models and chat interfaces from making my development start from zero every time. The question behind it is: How can I keep the project state in local files when models and interfaces such as GPT or Claude change?

## Not a finished workshop

This workshop is not a collection of finished projects. Many tools work, while some are in the archive because their implementation is not yet mature enough. What connects these projects, however, is my fascination with this technology and my desire to understand it through my own experiments. Building moves me into action: I have to make decisions and can observe their consequences.

This is how I work: begin with a question, build, look closely, and allow what I have built to change the original question. I do not have to wait for someone to tell me the next step.

Anyone looking for a finished method here will most likely not find one. Anyone who wants to begin with a small question of their own may find a trace, a tool, or an error they do not have to repeat. And I am left with the certainty that I get to live through a turbulent and singular period of AI.
