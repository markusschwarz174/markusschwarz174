# I build things to answer questions

I rarely begin with a fully formed question or a grand plan. Most of the time, I have a question or want to solve a problem, accompanied by a generous dose of curiosity and the desire to crack some kind of puzzle while building and to discover something new. I often notice how my own thinking changes and, with it, my perspective, my horizon, and ultimately I myself.

## Curiosity comes first

In July 2025, I wanted to compare two mathematics textbooks and use them to develop a plan for my class. It was four weeks before the new school year when I opened ChatGPT and asked: Can you help me? The answers were coherent and promising, and the comparison also produced learning objectives, tasks, and worksheets. But every answer put another possibility on the table. Soon I did not have too little material, but too much, and I had to select and leave things out instead of creating something new, as I had expected. Before long, I also had so many chats that the question arose: “How can I prevent useful thoughts from disappearing into old chats?”

I began structuring my prompts, and the sentences became categories and rules in YAML files. Not because I had been trained in this format, but because I use YAML in my smart home and it simply came to mind when I looked at school task formats. Over the following months, my work with AI continued to develop, and later I added things that had nothing to do with teaching at all: a food inventory, an email manager, an app for my accounts, and much more. They arise from everyday annoyances or simply from an idea for using AI to make a conventional task easier. Walk through your home and it is there; work in the kitchen and you will find it: the idea, the spark, the question.

## When structure makes things visible

It began with the simple observation that a YAML file can make teaching logic visible, and later other kinds of logic followed. Suddenly, it was no longer just about which content from which YAML files flowed into which worksheet templates. I wanted to know whether learning paths for different learning prerequisites could also be represented in this way.

For example, on one occasion I only wanted to improve a learning workshop and used concrete material from worksheets and examples. I had never planned to build anything larger. Nevertheless, the work produced new learning spirals, configurations, and didactic matrices unlike anything I had seen before. Even more important was what happened to my own thinking while I was building: my traditional view of teaching changed. Only by letting go of conventional assumptions could individual tasks become a multidimensional learning space in which tasks interact dynamically and learning paths become visible as temporary networks.

I had found my new virtual playground, and with AI I could explore ideas that I would never have been able to examine in this way on my own. AI could represent relationships and reveal gaps, and when I wanted to extend the model by a fifth dimension, GPT declared that the model could no longer be presented. I wanted to continue anyway.

The frequently asked question of how to separate the intellectual contributions of the human and AI is justified, but by the fifth turn at the latest, it can no longer be answered. There are too many small decisions, corrections, and detours. And that is not negligence, but the normal result of an iterative collaboration. The human influences the language model through their prompts, and the language model influences the human through its output. Was it a farsighted decision to require results involving AI to be labelled, or would it not have been better to devise a label for HUMAN-ONLY?

## And sometimes you become obsessed

With every project, the number of files, versions, and chats grew. The whole thing became less manageable, and I lost track of the connections. That is why I began exporting chats so that I could index, describe, and find them again later. Various forms of summaries, metadata, relationships, versions, and checks were later added to the keywords. In roughly 53 chat sessions, a library thus became a library system for my educational documents. That is not a number I would recommend as a working method, but it shows how quickly a tool can become a major project and how deeply you can become absorbed in a problem. I often worked late into the night because I believed that the next correction would provide the solution for the next step. Sometimes it did. Sometimes it did not. And sometimes I had to discard many hours of work because the approach had already been wrong.

It was the first time I had built an architecture with assistants, review reports, and versions within a project. And it was also the first time I had used AI to review AI-generated code by having it simulate the code.

It worked. The tool has since been expanded into a meta-index and later helped me find, among all those chats, the information I needed for my reconstructed project reports in FIELD NOTES.

## Learning without a curriculum

I am not a professional programmer, and I am not paid for this work either. I am a teacher, and there is no curriculum. No one requires me to account for the path I take. I can therefore decide for myself where to begin and what to do next. No one tells me: Start with prompt engineering, then learn Python, followed by databases, local servers, version control, and governance.

I began before I could do any of that. The next obstacle showed me what I needed to learn next. YAML, SQLite, NAMING.MD, AI_WHISPERER.MD, and much more emerged from precisely this process. Sometimes that was enough. Sometimes I had to go back and look more closely in order to understand what I had built. And sometimes AI built something I did not understand. Then I had it explained to me until I understood it.

The FIELD NOTES emerged when I began to become aware of this development. I reconstructed earlier projects from files and chats and looked not only for successes, but also for the places where something changed. My own collaboration with AI became the subject of investigation. Not to turn every mistake into a grand insight, but because I wanted to know why something had worked while something else had been a wrong turn.

I am grateful to have the freedom to take wrong turns and explore new possibilities. I am glad that politicians and authorities have not yet occupied this field. The still lightly regulated space around AI is the main reason why I am where I am today. But it is only a matter of time before supervisors and authorities begin to occupy this field of AI and label every last unclaimed space. It will then no longer be about learning, but about following instructions. It will mark the end of the pioneering era, and I imagine myself having to attend compulsory introductory courses on “proper prompting” in ten years’ time.

For now, however, the focus lies elsewhere, and fortunately I do not have to wait for someone to tell me how to use AI “correctly”; instead, AI helps me discover for myself what works. That also means understanding what my responsibility is and where it begins. Responsible use sometimes produces nothing more than a worksheet. Sometimes it produces a new question. Both are a good beginning for new learning.

## When the system itself becomes the problem

In the summer of 2026, I began transferring my development work to local tools for my everyday life. But one project fell out of balance. It developed into a system of rules, a runtime, a web interface, and ever more control mechanisms. I lost the overview and had to make an emergency stop. The old versions now sit in a folder named CONTAMINATED VERSIONS. I later rebuilt the project on a cleaner foundation, and it is in use today.

For the financial project that followed, I wanted to do better and relied on mutual review. A language model such as GPT was to build, while another such as Claude was to review. I carried start prompts and reports from one system to the other. The review reports appeared thorough, but I realised far too late that they did not use my original assignment as their reference. Instead, they used a mixture of my requirements and assumptions that an AI had added during development. The review system reported FULFILLED because it defined the wrong thing in the wrong place as correct.

That was an uncomfortable insight. I collected projects in which I had actually lost control and used them to develop a set of rules for my collaboration with AI. It now describes intention, boundaries, responsibilities, actions, and integrity. But more rules do not automatically mean more control. A system can fail because of its own safeguards. That is why another level now occupies me: How can a human–AI system observe what it knows, decides, does, learns, and ought to forget again? This is about a collaboration that does not merely produce results, but can distinguish intention, actions, and results from one another. I call this approach Metacognitive Engineering.

Alongside it, I am working on a local project memory that I call Open Project Memory. It is intended to prevent changing language models and chat interfaces from making my development start from zero every time. The question behind it is: How can I keep the project state in local files when models and interfaces such as GPT or Claude change?

## Not a finished workshop

This workshop is not a collection of finished projects. Many tools work, while some are in the archive because their implementation is not yet mature enough. What connects these projects is my fascination with AI as a technology and with its possible applications. It has become a passion to understand it through my own experiments. Building moves me into action, and I have to make decisions. In the end, I can observe the result.

Anyone looking for a finished method here will most likely not find one. Anyone who wants to begin with a small question of their own may find a trace, a tool, or an error they do not have to repeat.

And I remain aware that I have the privilege of living through a turbulent era of AI that is unique.

Markus
